UniKey Protocol

Buying Sneakers with UniKey

What happens when you tap "Buy Now" on a pair of Jordan 4s — and your phone proves you authorized the purchase without sharing a password, card number, or token.

The Scenario

You're buying $189 sneakers on a shopping app

You found the pair you want, you tap "Buy Now," and your phone handles the rest. No password. No card number sent over the wire. Your phone's secure chip signs a cryptographic proof that you approved this exact purchase — and that proof travels through the store and their payment processor before coming back to your phone for final confirmation.

The Flow

Step by step — what happens after you tap "Buy Now"

1

You tap "Buy Now" on the app

Your Phone → Store App

You're looking at Jordan 4 Retro, size 11, $189. You tap Buy. The app doesn't ask for a password or card number — it asks your phone to prove you're you.

2

Your phone asks for Face ID

Your Phone (Secure Enclave)

Your phone shows you the details: "Pay $189.00 to SoleSupply for Jordan 4 Retro?" You confirm with Face ID. Your phone's secure chip — the same hardware that protects Apple Pay — creates a Trust Packet.

What's a Trust Packet?

Think of it as a digital notarized receipt that says: "This person, on this device, approved this exact purchase, at this exact time, for this exact store." It's signed by hardware that can't be copied or faked remotely.

3

Your phone creates the Trust Packet

Your Phone → Store

The secure chip builds and signs the packet. This is what it looks like under the hood:

Trust Packet — Your Purchase Authorization
{
  "claims": {
    "audience":   "solesupply.com",
    "issuer":     "device:iphone-15-pro",
    "scope":      ["pay"],
    "subject":    "user@icloud.com"
  },
  "header": {
    "expires_at": "2026-03-16T12:05:00Z",   // good for 5 minutes
    "issued_at":  "2026-03-16T12:00:00Z",
    "nonce":      "a7f3c9e1-4b2d-4f8a-9c6e-3d5b7a2e1f09",
    "packet_id":  "c47e2d91-8a3f-4e5b-b7c1-9d6f0e4a2b83",
    "tp_version": "1.0"
  },
  "payload": {
    "amount":   "189.00",
    "currency": "USD",
    "item":     "Jordan 4 Retro - Size 11",
    "order_id": "SS-20260316-7829"
  },
  "signatures": [{
    "algorithm": "ed25519",
    "key_id":    "2026q1-user",
    "signature": "MEQCIG3k8xYw...7Hd2sF4Q",
    "signer":    "user@icloud.com"
  }]
}

What each field means

  • audience — This payment is only valid at SoleSupply. Intercept it, send it to another store? Rejected.
  • scope: "pay" — This packet only authorizes a payment. Nothing else.
  • payload — The exact amount, item, and order. Change a single character and the signature breaks.
  • expires_at — 5 minutes. After that, it's dead.
  • nonce — One-time use. Can never be replayed.
4

SoleSupply verifies your packet and co-signs it

Store (UniKey Verifier + Signer)

SoleSupply doesn't just blindly forward your request. They verify your Trust Packet first — checking your signature, the amount, and the time window. Once they confirm it's legitimate, they create their own delegation packet that says: "We, SoleSupply, have verified this customer's authorization and are now delegating payment processing to Stripe."

They sign this delegation with their own key, and it cryptographically references your original packet. The store never sees a card number because there isn't one — the Trust Packet is the authorization.

What SoleSupply verified

  • Is the user's signature valid? (look up public key via DNS)
  • Is the audience correct? (packet is addressed to solesupply.com — yes)
  • Is it still within the time window? (not expired)
  • Does the order match? ($189, Jordan 4 Retro, order SS-20260316-7829)
SoleSupply's Delegation Packet to Stripe
{
  "claims": {
    "delegated_to": "stripe.com",
    "issuer":       "solesupply.com",
    "parent_tph":   "sha256:a1b2c3d4...",  // links back to YOUR packet
    "scope":        ["pay:max_189"],
    "subject":      "order:SS-20260316-7829",
    "constraints": {
      "amount_max": 189.00,
      "currency":   "USD",
      "merchant":   "solesupply.com"
    }
  },
  "header": {
    "expires_at": "2026-03-16T12:03:00Z",   // even shorter — 3 min
    "issued_at":  "2026-03-16T12:00:30Z",
    "packet_id":  "d58f3ea2-9b4g-5f6c-c8d2-0e7g1f5b3c94",
    "tp_version": "1.0"
  },
  "signatures": [{
    "algorithm": "ed25519",
    "key_id":    "2026q1-solesupply",
    "signature": "MEUCIQDn9Bz...xR3pLmW",
    "signer":    "solesupply.com"
  }]
}

Why does the store sign too?

Because now Stripe doesn't have to just trust the store's API call. Stripe can see: the customer authorized $189 (signed by the customer's device), and the store verified that and delegated the charge (signed by the store's key). Two independent signatures. Two layers of accountability.

5

Stripe verifies the entire chain

Stripe (UniKey Verifier)

Stripe now has two Trust Packets — yours and SoleSupply's. They verify the full chain in milliseconds:

Stripe's verification checklist

  • Is the user's signature valid? (look up their public key via DNS)
  • Is SoleSupply's signature valid? (look up their public key via DNS)
  • Does SoleSupply's parent_tph correctly reference the user's packet? (hash must match exactly)
  • Is the scope valid? SoleSupply delegated pay:max_189 — that's a subset of the user's pay scope. Good.
  • Are the time windows valid? SoleSupply's 3-min expiry is shorter than the user's 5-min. Good.
  • Has this nonce been used before? (replay check)
6

Stripe sends confirmation back to your phone

Stripe → Store → Your Phone

Payment is authorized. Your phone gets a confirmation: "$189.00 paid to SoleSupply — Jordan 4 Retro." The sneakers are on their way.

The whole thing took about 2 seconds from the Face ID scan.

7

Every party can independently prove what happened

Auditable by anyone, anytime

You, SoleSupply, Stripe, or a bank auditor can take the Trust Packets and independently verify the entire chain. No one needs to call anyone. The cryptography proves it.

The Delegation Chain

How authority flowed and narrowed

Your phone started with full authority. Each handoff could only keep or reduce that authority — never expand it.

Your Phone — Root Authority
"issuer":     "your phone (secure enclave)"
"scope":      ["pay"]
"amount":     "189.00"
"expires":    "5 minutes"
"audience":   "solesupply.com"
"parent":     none — you are the root
▼ store can only delegate what you gave them
SoleSupply — Verifies your packet, then delegates to Stripe
"verified":    "user's signature, audience, amount, time window"
"delegated_to": "stripe.com"
"scope":        ["pay:max_189"]           // can't exceed your amount
"expires":      "3 minutes"              // shorter than yours
"parent":       "hash of YOUR packet"    // cryptographic link to you
"signed_by":   "solesupply.com"         // store puts their name on it
▼ Stripe verifies BOTH signatures — yours and the store's — then processes
Stripe — Verifies full chain, then executes payment
"chain":       "your phone → solesupply → stripe"
"verified":    "both signatures valid, scope valid, not expired, not replayed"
"action":      "charge $189.00"
"result":      "AUTHORIZED"
▼ confirmation flows back
Your Phone — Confirmation
"status":   "Payment complete"
"amount":   "$189.00"
"merchant": "SoleSupply"
"item":     "Jordan 4 Retro - Size 11"
So What?

Why this is different from how payments work today

Today

  • You type in a card number — it gets stored on servers everywhere
  • A stolen card number works from anywhere in the world
  • Passwords and OTPs can be phished
  • The store and processor have to "trust" each other via contracts
  • Chargebacks are he-said-she-said

With UniKey

  • No card number ever leaves your phone
  • Fraud requires physically stealing your phone + beating Face ID
  • Nothing to phish — authorization is hardware-bound
  • Trust is cryptographic, not contractual
  • Disputes are provable — the math settles it
